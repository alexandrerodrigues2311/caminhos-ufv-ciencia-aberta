University commuting and stated willingness to change travel mode in Viçosa, Brazil: a reproducible framework for a revised doctoral survey

Methodological demonstration using 400 synthetic records. Draft for scientific review, not a completed empirical study or a submission-ready manuscript. Questionnaire: ufv-mobilidade-4.2.0. The English and Portuguese documents describe the same study.

Abstract

University commuting connects household constraints, daily routines and destinations within a campus. This study develops a versioned workflow for a doctoral investigation of mobility and cycling-route planning in Viçosa, Brazil. The revised survey distinguishes habitual outbound travel, return-trip asymmetry, willingness to change mode, six hypothetical conditions and mode-specific perceptions. A deterministic dataset of 400 artificial records demonstrates descriptive analysis, internal predictive validation, repeated-response analysis and exploratory interaction modelling. The predictive analysis includes 384 observations and excludes 16 missing outcomes. Models are compared against a prevalence baseline using out-of-fold predictions. Geographic outputs separate approximate origins, documented campus landmarks and marks on the official campus-map image. Synthetic results verify computation and cannot establish travel demand, policy effectiveness or population associations. The prospective contribution is an explicit connection between measurement, admissible inference and geographic information needed for cycling-route planning.

Keywords: university mobility; active travel; stated choices; predictive validation; spatial information; synthetic data.

1. Introduction

A university campus generates recurrent travel while bringing together people with different resources, schedules and residential locations. These differences matter because a preferred transport mode may not be a feasible alternative. Describing current cyclists alone therefore cannot explain which constraints prevent other university members from using active modes, nor identify the corridors where intervention would be useful.

The doctoral project concerns cycling-route planning in Viçosa. Its digital survey must connect the experience of walkers, public-transport users, drivers, passengers and cyclists. An inclusive invitation is substantively relevant: recruiting primarily existing cyclists would restrict the evidence available about unmet needs. Participation remains voluntary and most items may be left unanswered.

The current contribution is methodological rather than a claim about observed policy effects. We ask how a revised survey can preserve the distinction between existing behaviour, stated willingness, hypothetical choice and geographic context, while supporting reproducible analysis. Empirical collection and validation are required before local planning recommendations can be defended.

2. Literature and conceptual framework

Lodi et al. (2024) analysed university commuting in Urbino using mixed multinomial logit and latent-class models. Their study motivates attention to heterogeneity rather than a single representative commuter. Kim and Lee (2023) used an integrated choice and latent-variable model, illustrating that attitudinal interpretation requires an explicit measurement framework. Kamkar et al. (2025) compared discrete-choice and machine-learning approaches in university commuting, providing a reference for distinguishing behavioural interpretation from predictive performance.

These approaches inform the present workflow without supplying coefficients to its synthetic generator. Current mode is an observed self-report, willingness is a stated disposition, and scenario choices are responses to hypothetical conditions. None is interchangeable with adoption following a real intervention. Similarly, a link between an approximate residential origin and a campus landmark is not an observed route through the street network.

Three prospective hypotheses organize the empirical stage: H1, individual and current travel characteristics are associated with willingness to change; H2, an individual's choices differ across the six hypothetical conditions; and H3, the association between cycling experience and willingness differs by current active-mode use. These hypotheses require refinement before evaluation of real data. They are not confirmed by the artificial dataset.

3. Materials and methods

3.1. Setting and measurement

The intended population comprises adults connected to the UFV campus in Viçosa. The project anticipates 400 participants. The current demonstration instead contains 400 artificial records generated for interface and computational testing. Synthetic collection dates are in September 2026; they do not establish the official empirical fieldwork period.

Questionnaire 4.2 records profile, housing, available vehicles, cycling experience, habitual origin, campus attendance and schedules, first destination, outbound mode, choice priorities, internal travel and return-mode differences. Willingness to change is followed by conditional items on the chance of change or barriers. Six situations are evaluated separately. Safety, comfort and practicality are rated by mode, with an explicit unable-to-assess option. These dimensions are not assumed to form a validated latent scale.

The revision corrects an instruction that previously directed all existing active-mode users to reject change. They can now indicate a different active alternative. The official July 2026 campus map supplies 107 named locations, searchable without knowing their numbers. A mark on that map has image coordinates; it is not silently transformed into latitude and longitude.

3.2. Data processing and synthetic design

Records retain unique identifiers, questionnaire and design versions, timestamps and explicit synthetic/demo flags. The workflow rejects incompatible versions, duplicated identifiers and mixed real and test data. A seed of 8421264 produces a reproducible demonstration with arbitrary distributions. The sample is not calibrated to UFV demographics and does not reproduce observed associations. Conditional questions follow their parent responses and missing values remain distinguishable from inapplicability.

Descriptive denominators use valid responses for each item. For multiple selection, percentages are calculated per respondent and may sum to more than 100%. Geography is optional. Device location requires an explicit action and confirmation, and approximate points are rounded to a 0.002-degree grid. This reduces detail but cannot guarantee anonymity.

3.3. Prediction

The binary outcome is willingness to change in qChange. Age, institutional role, income category, housing, cycling experience and current mode form the predictor set. Conditional chance and barrier items are excluded to avoid encoding the outcome in its predictors. Median numeric imputation, scaling and categorical encoding are fitted inside each of five stratified training folds. Each person appears once in this analysis; identifiers are unique, so folds cannot share a participant.

A prevalence classifier, logistic regression and a shallow random forest use fixed hyperparameters. Out-of-fold balanced accuracy, AUC, Brier score and log loss are reported. No hyperparameter search is conducted on the test folds. Results provide internal validation only; external performance, uncertainty in model comparisons and decision utility require further evaluation.

3.4. Repeated scenarios and exploratory moderation

Scenario alternatives are retained descriptively and additionally reduced to changing versus maintaining the current mode. Selecting the current mode by name is coded as maintenance. A binomial generalized estimating equation uses scenario indicators, participant clusters, exchangeable working correlation and robust variance. Scenario 1 is the reference. This is not a randomized discrete-choice experiment and does not estimate causal effects, willingness to pay or elasticities.

An exploratory logistic interaction relates willingness to knowing how to cycle, current active-mode use and their interaction, adjusted for age in decades. Cell-size criteria prevent adjustment in very sparse recuts. The cycling item partly combines ability and use frequency, limiting interpretation. The specification is an initial association model; additional confounding and sensitivity analyses must be planned. Causal mediation is not estimated because temporal ordering and appropriate measurement are absent.

4.3. Geographic representation

Street geometry and campus landmarks represent real places. Demonstration origins are invented and explicitly marked as synthetic. The dashboard displays aggregate origins and links only when the corresponding group contains at least five observations. Unmatched destinations remain unlocated. The official campus-map view separately aggregates image marks. Neither display infers an individual's travelled path.

5. Discussion

The workflow makes analytical scope inspectable. A classifier that fails to improve on prevalence under the selected metrics provides no evidence of predictive gain, even if overall accuracy looks high in an imbalanced dataset. An imprecise interaction cannot establish similarity between groups. Synthetic coefficients are computational outputs under the generator, not a basis for accepting the empirical hypotheses.

The prospective substantive contribution is to evaluate whether connecting travel constraints, willingness and validated geographic information identifies needs overlooked by counting current cyclists alone. That proposition requires observed data, a defensible definition of planning priorities and comparison against simpler rules. Adding a larger collection of charts is not itself evidence of scientific novelty.

Important design limits remain. Bundled infrastructure scenarios do not isolate parking, charging and changing-room components. Unspecified parking charges and fare increases do not support monetary sensitivity estimates. A cross-sectional perception may partly reflect the mode already used. Follow-up observation would be needed to study the intention–behaviour relationship. A separate experimental design would be needed to attribute policy effects.

5.1. Bias and regional transfer

Voluntary online recruitment may select people with stronger mobility interests or easier digital access. Composition by institutional role should be compared with appropriate administrative totals before considering weighting; matching observed margins cannot remove unobserved selection. Missingness patterns and analytic exclusions should be reported by group. A target of 400 does not guarantee stable estimates in many intersections of mode, role and income.

Viçosa's terrain motivates directional route analysis, but elevation and network distance cannot be inferred from a city label. Street-level slope, crossings, lighting, traffic stress and feasible routes require additional geographic data or field audits. Approximate origins also introduce positional uncertainty. Sensitivity to aggregation and location error must be assessed before prioritizing corridors.

5.2. Limitations of the demonstration

A single deterministic synthetic dataset is not a Monte Carlo validation study. It cannot establish estimator bias, interval coverage or statistical power. The current models have no external validation and the interaction adjustment is limited. The questionnaire does not validate latent constructs by itself. These limits motivate a preregistered empirical analysis and a broader simulation study rather than stronger claims from the existing artificial data.

6. Conclusions

The revised platform connects a doctoral mobility survey to version-aware processing, descriptive monitoring, internally validated prediction, repeated-response models and documented geographic displays. The demonstration verifies the tested computations while identifying what the instrument cannot establish. Empirical collection, measurement review, network information and sensitivity analysis are required before drawing conclusions about cycling demand or intervention priorities in Viçosa.
